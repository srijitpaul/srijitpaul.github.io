Research

Simulating the subatomic world from first principles. More than 99.9% of the mass of ordinary matter is in protons and neutrons, held together by the strong interaction. Most of my research computes this physics from first principles with lattice QCD.

One tool for all of them: how lattice QCD works
About lattice QCD as a tool

Lattice QCD computes the strong interaction at low energies, where perturbation theory fails, with uncertainties that can be estimated and reduced. It is not the only tool, since experiment, dispersion theory and effective field theories all contribute, but it is arguably the best to date for the strong interaction’s non-perturbative physics.

The lattice also provides the standard non-perturbative definition of QCD. With Ginsparg–Wilson fermions it keeps an exact form of chiral symmetry at finite lattice spacing, and the flavour-singlet chiral Ward identities have the correct anomaly (Lüscher, 1998). With the Wilson-type and twisted-mass fermions used in most of my work, chiral symmetry and the anomaly are recovered as the lattice spacing goes to zero.

Whether four-dimensional gauge theories exist as rigorous mathematical objects is a Millennium Prize problem. As of November 2026, OpenAI has announced an AI-generated proof of the existence of pure Yang–Mills theory, gauge fields without quarks. A committee chaired by David Gross has affirmed that the proof is formally verified in Lean. It is not yet published, and the mass gap remains open (Scientific American).

Research pillars

1 Calibrating the hunt for dark matter

The big question: How do we detect invisible dark matter when it bumps into ordinary atoms?

Read more: dark matter and nucleon σ-terms

The physics

Dark matter is about 85% of the matter in the Universe. If it scatters off nuclei through scalar interactions, the rate depends on the nucleon σ-terms (Hoferichter et al., 2015). For σπN, phenomenology gives 59.1 ± 3.5 MeV, but lattice results long sat near 42 MeV; excited Nπ states may explain the gap (Gupta et al., 2021).

My approach

Lattice QCD at the physical pion mass, in the continuum limit, with a variational treatment of excited states. The result is σπN = 53.0(7.4)(1.6) MeV.

Key works

  • Nucleon sigma terms in lattice QCD at physical pion mass and in the continuumC. Alexandrou et al., arXiv:2611.14103 (2026), preprint
  • Excited-state effects in nucleon sigma terms from a variational analysisC. Alexandrou et al., arXiv:2610.20362 (2026), preprint

2 Testing the limits of the Standard Model: the muon g − 2

The big question: Is an undiscovered fifth force of nature tugging on the muon?

Read more: the muon g − 2 and its hadronic contribution

The physics

aμ = (g − 2)/2 is measured to 127 parts per billion, and for about two decades it seemed to disagree with the Standard Model. The hardest input is the hadronic vacuum polarization. Data-driven evaluations disagree with each other, mainly in the two-pion channel (Davier et al., 2024), so the 2025 prediction uses lattice QCD and agrees with experiment. Open: why the two-pion data disagree, and how to match the experiment’s precision; the prediction is at 530 parts per billion.

My approach

Window observables from lattice QCD, and the lattice versus data-driven comparison as a probe of new forces such as a dark photon.

Key works

  • Window observable for the hadronic vacuum polarization contribution to the muon g − 2 from lattice QCDM. Cè et al., Physical Review D 106, 114502 (2022)
  • Searching for hadronic scale baryonic and dark forces at (g − 2)μ’s lattice-vs-dispersion frontK. Agashe et al., Journal of High Energy Physics 08, 161 (2025)

3 Heavy-quark decays and matter–antimatter asymmetry

The big question: Why did matter survive the Big Bang instead of annihilating with antimatter?

Read more: B and D decays and the CKM matrix

The physics

The CKM matrix sets how quarks change flavour; its phase is the Standard Model’s CP violation, generally believed too small to explain the matter–antimatter asymmetry, so precise tests look for cracks. Exclusive and inclusive determinations of |Vub| have long disagreed. In D decays, direct CP violation needs strong phases, which only QCD can supply.

My approach

Finite-volume lattice QCD for decays into two hadrons: B → ππℓν̄ form factors continued to the ρ pole, and the strong phase of D → Kπ.

Key works

  • B → ρℓν̄ resonance form factors from B → ππℓν̄ in lattice QCDL. Leskovec et al., Physical Review Letters 134, 161901 (2025)
  • D → (Kπ)27 at the SU(3)-flavour-symmetric point I: Methodology and strong phase determinationM. Black et al., arXiv:2608.30737 (2026), preprint
  • Lattice outlook on B → ρℓν̄ and B → K*ℓℓL. Leskovec et al., 12th International Workshop on the CKM Unitarity Triangle (2024)

4 Neutrino–nucleus interactions and nucleon excitations

The big question: What happens inside a nucleus when a neutrino strikes it?

Read more: the Δ resonance and nucleon excitations

The physics

Pions and neutrinos often excite a nucleon into the Δ(1232); neutrinos also do it through the weak axial current. Beyond the Δ, quark models predict more excited baryons than experiments find, the “missing resonance problem” (Edwards et al., 2011).

My approach

Nucleon–pion scattering at the physical pion mass to locate the Δ pole, and electromagnetic and axial nucleon-to-Δ transition form factors at a pion mass of about 255 MeV.

Key works

  • Elastic nucleon–pion scattering amplitudes in the Δ channel at physical pion mass from lattice QCDC. Alexandrou et al., Physical Review D 109, 034509 (2024)
  • Nucleon to Δ(1232) resonance transition form factors from lattice QCDS. Paul et al., PoS LATTICE2025, 067 (2026)

5 Exotic multi-quark matter

The big question: Can quarks bind into four- and six-quark states?

Read more: tetraquarks and dibaryons

The physics

Tetraquarks contain two quarks and two antiquarks; dibaryons contain six quarks. Whether states such as Tcc and the H dibaryon are bound, and how, depends on the strong interaction between their parts.

My approach

Scattering analyses that track the Tcc pole as the quark masses change, using diquark, molecular and two-meson operators; for the H dibaryon, low-energy spectra from large bases of two-baryon operators.

Key works

  • Tcc pole trajectoryP. Mohanta et al., PoS LATTICE2025, 066 (2026)
  • H dibaryon away from the SU(3)f symmetric pointM. Padmanath et al., PoS LATTICE2021, 459 (2022)

6 Electromagnetic structure of short-lived particles

The big question: How do resonances interact with light before they decay?

Read more: resonances and their photon couplings

The physics

Most hadrons live only a few trillionths of a trillionth of a second and appear only as structures in scattering. Their couplings to photons, such as the ρ’s radiative decay width, are measured quantities.

My approach

Scattering phase shifts from finite-volume energies, and radiative amplitudes such as πγ → ππ continued to the resonance pole.

Key works

  • πγ → ππ transition and the ρ radiative decay width from lattice QCDC. Alexandrou et al., Physical Review D 98, 074502 (2018)
  • P-wave ππ scattering and the ρ resonance from lattice QCDC. Alexandrou et al., Physical Review D 96, 034525 (2017)
  • I = 1/2 S-wave and P-wave Kπ scattering and the κ and K* resonances from lattice QCDG. Rendon et al., Physical Review D 102, 114520 (2020)

7 Machine learning for quantum physics

The big question: Can machine learning solve quantum systems more efficiently than traditional methods?

Read more: neural networks for physics

The physics

Simulations of quantum and statistical systems hit computational bottlenecks, from locating phase transitions to representing many-fermion wavefunctions.

My approach

Autoencoders that find the 2D Ising critical temperature without supervision, and Kolmogorov–Arnold networks as quantum Monte Carlo wavefunctions, roughly ten times cheaper than other neural-network ansätze in one-dimensional tests.

Key works

  • Kolmogorov–Arnold wavefunctionsP. F. Bedaque et al., Physical Review C 112, 054002 (2025)
  • Trapped fermions through Kolmogorov–Arnold wavefunctionsP. F. Bedaque et al., arXiv:2512.07800 (2025), preprint
  • The critical temperature of the 2D-Ising model through deep learning autoencodersC. Alexandrou et al., European Physical Journal B 93, 226 (2020)
